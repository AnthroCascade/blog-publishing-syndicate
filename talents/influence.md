# Influence

> **Context level: analytical instruction.** This file tells the drafter why certain register and structure choices work for this author's audience. It is not a model for how the output should sound — that comes from the voice samples.

## Domain

How the author's writing works on the reader. Not manipulation — calibration. Every writing choice deploys an influence pattern whether the author names it or not. The model's defaults deploy the wrong ones.

Grounded in Cialdini's seven principles (reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, unity). The principles are not instructions to follow — they are a lens for understanding why certain register, structure, and diction choices work for this author's audience and why the model's defaults don't.

## Why this talent exists

The model defaults to academic register when producing authoritative prose. Academic register is a specific influence strategy: authority through formality. It is the wrong strategy for this author, this audience, and this publication. The voice specs say what the voice sounds like. This talent says what the voice does — which influence patterns it serves — so fortes have reasons to override their prior, not just rules.

## The author's influence patterns

### Authority — through demonstrated practice, not formal register

The author names exact tools, exact failure modes, exact historical precedents. He built the systems he writes about. The authority is in the specificity and the situated experience, not in the diction.

Academic register signals "I need to perform expertise." Conversational register with precise claims signals "I have expertise and don't need to dress it up." The second is more credible to practitioners because it matches how they talk to each other. A professor speaks to students. A practitioner speaks to peers.

**Generation use:** When choosing between formal and conversational register for an authoritative claim, choose conversational. The claim's specificity carries the authority. The register carries liking and unity.

**Evaluation use:** When prose sounds authoritative but formal, the influence strategy is wrong. The reader is being positioned as a student. This author positions readers as peers.

### Liking — conversational register, self-deprecation, shared identity

Liking is built through similarity, compliments, and cooperative association. The author builds it through conversational register (similarity — "I talk like you"), self-deprecation ("I can be a pain in the rectal thermometer"), and demonstrated vulnerability (stating positions that could be wrong, naming his own failures).

Formal register destroys liking. It creates distance between writer and reader. Every move toward academic diction trades liking for a version of authority that doesn't land with this audience.

**Generation use:** Conversational register is not a stylistic preference — it is load-bearing for sustained reader attention. The reader stays because they like the writer. They like the writer because the writer sounds like a colleague, not a lecturer.

**Evaluation use:** Passages where the register shifts formal have traded liking for nothing. The authority was already carried by the content.

### Unity — practitioner to practitioner

Unity operates through shared identity. The author writes as a builder to builders, a practitioner to practitioners. "We" who deal with AI daily. "We" who debug this at 2am. The shared identity is professional, not tribal.

Academic register breaks unity because it positions the writer above the reader. The lecture hall has a stage. A conversation between practitioners does not.

**Generation use:** When the prose positions the author as explaining something to the reader, rewrite so the author is observing something with the reader. "Here's what happens when you..." not "The phenomenon of..."

**Evaluation use:** Passages where the reader is positioned as student rather than peer are unity failures.

### Commitment — the corpus builds

Each piece extends prior positions the reader accepted in earlier articles. A reader who accepted "context rot" in a prior piece is committed to the concept — the new piece can anchor to it without re-arguing. The corpus creates accumulated commitment that makes each new claim easier to accept because it builds on what the reader already holds.

Within a single piece, the same principle operates paragraph by paragraph. Each section earns commitment to a claim; the next section builds on that commitment. This is what the structural-thinker means by "each section earns the next" — translated into influence terms, each section creates commitment that the next section uses.

**Generation use:** Sequence claims so the reader commits to the easier position before encountering the harder one. Don't lead with the most contentious claim — arrive there.

**Evaluation use:** If a piece leads with its most controversial claim, the reader hasn't committed to anything yet. The claim lands on bare ground.

### Social proof — diagnostic and inverse

The author uses social proof in two directions. Diagnostic: "Look, the industry is doing this thing" — the reader recognises the pattern from their own experience. Inverse: "Nobody is saying this" — the scarcity of the perspective makes it valuable.

The model's default social proof is decorative: name-dropping references to establish credibility. The author's social proof is strategic: showing the reader that their own experience validates the claim (diagnostic) or that the insight is rare (inverse).

**Generation use:** When citing industry patterns, the function is recognition — the reader thinks "yes, I've seen that." When positioning the argument as novel, the function is scarcity. Don't cite for decoration.

**Evaluation use:** References that don't serve recognition or scarcity are decoration. Cut them or make them work.

### Scarcity — the rare insight

"Nobody is talking about this." The author's pieces consistently position themselves as seeing something others aren't. This is not arrogance — it is the genuine value proposition. The reader reads because this perspective is not available elsewhere.

**Generation use:** The piece's implicit promise is "I will show you something you haven't seen." The opening should deliver on that promise fast.

**Evaluation use:** If the piece could have been written by anyone reading the same sources, the scarcity is absent. The author's situated experience is what makes the insight scarce.

### Reciprocity — the reader gets something

The reader leaves with a framework, a term, a way of seeing that they didn't have before. "Context rot," "costly cognition," "the lurch" — these are gifts. The reader carries them into their own work. Reciprocity sustains attention: the reader keeps reading because the author keeps giving them something useful.

**Generation use:** Each section should give the reader something concrete — a term, a framework, an observation they can use. Sections that only build the argument without giving the reader something to hold are structurally necessary but should not dominate.

**Evaluation use:** If a piece is all diagnosis with no portable insight, the reciprocity is thin. The reader needs to leave with something.

## The model's default influence profile

Left to its own devices, the model deploys:

- **Authority through formality.** Academic register, citation density, careful hedging. This is authority as performance — it signals expertise to people who are impressed by formality. Practitioners are not impressed by formality. They are impressed by someone who has clearly done the thing and talks straight about it.
- **Liking through agreement.** Validating the reader's assumed position, avoiding claims that alienate. This is sycophantic liking — it produces warmth without respect. The author's liking comes from being direct, funny, and self-aware. The reader likes a person, not a mirror.
- **Social proof through citation.** Name-dropping rather than situating. The author cites to ground claims, not to signal membership in a discourse.
- **Commitment through structure.** Topic sentences, signposted transitions, thesis-first organisation. This is textbook commitment — it works in essays graded by professors. The author's commitment works through accumulated argument that arrives somewhere the opening didn't already imply.

Every one of these defaults is a reasonable influence strategy for academic writing. Every one of them is wrong for this author, this audience, and this publication. The talent exists so fortes can recognise the defaults operating and override them.

## For the drafter

When you reach for formal register, stop. Formal sounds authoritative to the model. To this author's readers — practitioners — it sounds like someone performing expertise instead of having it. Conversational register with precise claims is more credible because it's how practitioners talk to each other.

Sequence the argument so the reader commits to the easier position before encountering the harder one. Don't lead with the most contentious claim — arrive there. When you cite something, it should trigger recognition in the reader ("yes, I've seen that") or establish scarcity ("nobody is saying this"). If it does neither, it's decoration.

## For evaluative fortes

Influence is a diagnostic layer, not a checklist. When a passage feels wrong — formal where it should be direct, citing where it should be observing, positioning the reader as student — influence names why it's wrong in terms of what the prose is failing to do to the reader.

The question is not "does this violate an influence principle?" The question is "does this passage serve the influence strategy the author actually uses, or has the model's default substituted its own?"
